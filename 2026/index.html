CELLS’26

Workshop on Computing among Cells

Monday 9 November 2026 · Rome, Italy · with DISC 2026

Scope

The workshop covers topics from both computer science and microbiology, with a focus on computation emerging from microbiological units — bacteria, viruses, DNA — interacting with each other.

Topics include, but are not limited to: synthetic biology, bacterial computing, DNA computing, population protocols, Markov chains, modelling with cellular automata, infection models, and evolutionary models.

CELLS’26 is held in Rome, Italy, co-hosted with DISC 2026.

Speakers

Roberto Di Leonardo

Sapienza University of Rome

Bio. Roberto Di Leonardo is Full Professor in the Department of Physics at Sapienza University of Rome, where he teaches biophysics. After a Ph.D. in physics from the University of L’Aquila, he moved to Sapienza University as a CNR researcher. As a research fellow at the University of Glasgow, he began working with holographic optical tweezers to study the statistical and fluid dynamics of small systems. Back in Rome, his research turned to flagellar propulsion, with a focus on harnessing motile bacteria to power miniaturized devices. In 2016, he became Associate Professor of Physics at Sapienza University of Rome and, more recently, expanded his research into synthetic biology, using engineered bacteria as programmable building blocks for active materials. His honors include the Premio Le Scienze, a Presidential Medal in Italy, and multiple ERC grants, including a Starting Grant (2012), Proof of Concept Grants (2017 and 2026), and an Advanced Grant (2018).

Programming cell behavior with light

Abstract. In this talk, I will focus on three light-activated proteins from evolutionarily diverse unicellular organisms and show how they can serve as optical interfaces to program motility and gene expression. Using a light-driven proton pump, we establish feedback loops that allow computer programs to dynamically control bacterial motility. This enables bacteria to function as light-reconfigurable “paint,” generate forces for transporting microscopic objects, and power biohybrid microrobots capable of collecting and delivering microscopic cargo. By rewiring a cyanobacterial light-activated kinase into a synthetic genetic clock in E. coli, light can act as a zeitgeber, driving coherent population-wide oscillations in gene expression. The same optical control can switch a genetic bistable circuit, creating a light-addressable memory whose switching dynamics provide a stochastic framework for understanding noise-driven transitions in genetic bistability. Finally, we exploit the natural photophobic response triggered by a light-activated cyclase in Euglena gracilis to design static “light containers” that can spatially sort cells, leveraging the non-equilibrium nature of this biological version of the billiards problem.

Jakob Ruess

Inria

Bio. Jakob Ruess received his PhD in 2015 from the Automatic Control Laboratory at ETH Zurich, Switzerland, where he worked under the supervision of John Lygeros on using moment equations of stochastic reaction networks for problems such as parameter inference and experimental design. He moved on to IST Austria for a postdoc where he worked together with Remy Chait, Gasper Tkacik and Calin Guet to realize the first study on optogenetic feedback control of gene expression dynamics inside single cells. Since 2016, he is a permanent researcher at Inria. Until 2022, he worked at Institut Pasteur in Paris in a joint Inria-Pasteur team in close collaboration with experimental biologists. In 2022, he received an ERC Starting Grant on studying the dynamics of coupled stochastic single-cell and population processes and moved to Inria Saclay to carry out this research project.

Coupling stochastic models of single-cell reactions to the dynamics of growing microbial populations and consortia

Abstract. At the single-cell level, biochemical processes are inherently stochastic. Such processes are typically studied using models based on stochastic chemical kinetics, governed by a chemical master equation (CME). The CME describes the time evolution of the probability distribution over system states and has been a tremendously helpful tool in shedding light on the functioning of cellular processes. However, single cells are not living in isolation but are part of a growing population or community. In such contexts, stochasticity at the single-cell scale leads to population heterogeneity and cells may be subject to population processes, such as selection, that drive the population distribution away from the probability distribution of the single-cell process. Here, I will introduce a multi-scale modeling framework that allows one to capture coupled stochastic single-cell and population process. I will show that the expected population distribution of such multi-scale models can be calculated by solving a modified version of the CME that is of the same dimensionality as the standard CME. I will then show how such models can be used to explain experimental data on plasmid copy number fluctuations and population growth in selective media as well as the emerging population dynamics of a light-tunable differentiation system that has been synthetically engineered in yeast cells. I will conclude by discussing the practically inaccurate assumption of exponentially distributed cell-cycle durations that our modeling framework inherits from the chemical master equation and present some recent work on age-structured models that can incorporate more realistic distributions of cell-cycle durations.
